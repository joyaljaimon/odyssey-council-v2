## Book 1: The Gods in Council—Minerva’s Visit to Ithaca

The gods convene on Olympus to discuss the return of Odysseus, who is detained by Calypso on Ogygia. Zeus notes the folly of mortals, citing Aegisthus’s death as a warning, while Athena pleads for her favorite hero. Zeus agrees to facilitate Odysseus’s homecoming, noting Poseidon’s lingering wrath for the blinding of Polyphemus. Athena descends to Ithaca disguised as Mentes to advise Telemachus, who is distraught by the suitors consuming his estate. She encourages him to assert his authority, demand the suitors leave, and travel to Pylos and Sparta to seek news of his father. Telemachus, emboldened by the goddess, confronts the insolent suitors, specifically Antinous and Eurymachus, declaring his intent to rule his own house. Meanwhile, Penelope is distressed by the bard Phemius’s song of the Achaeans' return, but Telemachus firmly asserts his role as master, ordering her to retire. The suitors continue their revelry until nightfall. Telemachus retires to his chamber, guided by his nurse Euryclea, and spends the night reflecting on Athena’s counsel and his impending journey to discover his father’s fate.

People: Athena (advises Telemachus), Telemachus (seeks his father's legacy), Penelope (mourns Odysseus), Zeus (authorizes the return), Poseidon (opposes the return), Antinous/Eurymachus (arrogant suitors).

Ideas: Xenia, Nostos, Fate, Authority.

## Book 2: The Assembly and Departure

Telemachus convenes the first Ithacan assembly since the departure of Odysseus. He laments the ruin of his estate by the suitors who refuse to leave his home or seek Penelope’s hand properly. Antinous blames Penelope’s cunning, specifically her ruse of the shroud, for their presence. Telemachus refuses to expel his mother, invoking the gods and threatening the suitors with divine retribution. Zeus sends two eagles as an omen, which Halitherses interprets as a sign of Odysseus’s imminent return, though Eurymachus mocks the prophecy. Telemachus requests a ship to seek news of his father in Pylos and Sparta. Despite Mentor’s rebuke of the passive Ithacan elders, the assembly dissolves without support. Athena, disguised as Mentor, encourages Telemachus, promising to secure a crew and ship. While the suitors feast, Telemachus instructs his nurse, Euryclea, to prepare provisions in secret. That night, Athena casts a deep sleep over the drunken suitors, gathers a crew, and guides Telemachus to the harbor. They set sail under a fair wind provided by the goddess, beginning the young prince’s quest to restore his father’s honor and secure his own future.

People: Telemachus (seeks his father and maturity), Athena (guides and empowers Telemachus), Antinous and Eurymachus (arrogant, predatory suitors), Halitherses (prophet of doom for the suitors), Euryclea (loyal nurse).

Ideas: Nostos, Xenia (violated by the suitors), Fate, Divine Intervention.

QUOTES:

- (2.7) "We will not go back to our lands, neither here nor elsewhere, till she has made her choice and married some one or other of us."

## Book 3: Telemachus Visits Nestor at Pylos

Telemachus and Athena arrive at Pylos, where Nestor and his people are sacrificing to Poseidon. Athena encourages the hesitant Telemachus to approach the aged king for news of his father. Nestor welcomes them with traditional xenia, providing food and wine before inquiring about their identity. Telemachus reveals his mission, and Nestor recounts the hardships of the Trojan War, the dispersal of the Achaean fleet, and the tragic fate of Agamemnon, who was murdered by Aegisthus. Nestor urges Telemachus to be brave like Orestes, who avenged his father. He advises the youth to visit Menelaus in Sparta for further information. That night, Athena reveals her divinity by transforming into an eagle and flying away, leaving the Pylians in awe. Nestor, recognizing the goddess’s favor, vows a sacrifice. The next morning, the Pylians perform an elaborate ritual to honor Athena. Following the feast, Nestor provides his son Pisistratus to accompany Telemachus by chariot to Lacedaemon. They travel through the countryside, stopping at Pherae, and continue their journey the following day, moving closer to the answers Telemachus seeks regarding the fate of Odysseus.

People: Telemachus (searching for his father), Athena (guiding and protecting Telemachus), Nestor (wise king providing counsel), Pisistratus (Nestor’s son, companion to Telemachus).

Ideas: Xenia, nostos, divine intervention, duty/lineage.

QUOTES:

- (3.11) "I seek news of my unhappy father Ulysses, who is said to have sacked the town of Troy in company with yourself."
- (3.16) "See what a good thing it is for a man to leave a son behind him to do as Orestes did, who killed false Aegisthus the murderer of his noble father."
- (3.30) "I see that you are going to be a great hero some day, since the gods wait upon you thus while you are still so young."

## Book 4: The Visit to King Menelaus

Telemachus and Pisistratus reach Lacedaemon, where King Menelaus is celebrating the double wedding of his children. Menelaus welcomes them with lavish xenia, and upon recognizing Telemachus’s resemblance to Odysseus, all weep for the lost hero. Helen joins them, drugging their wine with an Egyptian herb to banish sorrow. Menelaus recounts his own arduous return from Troy, detailing how he trapped the sea-god Proteus to learn his fate. Proteus revealed that Agamemnon was murdered by Aegisthus and that Odysseus is currently held captive by the nymph Calypso on her island. Menelaus offers Telemachus a gift, which the youth declines, preferring a piece of plate over horses unsuitable for Ithaca. Meanwhile, back in Ithaca, the suitors discover Telemachus has secretly sailed to Pylos. Enraged, they plot to ambush and murder him upon his return. Medon informs Penelope of the conspiracy, plunging her into despair. She is only comforted when Athena sends a phantom of her sister, Iphthime, to assure her that her son is under divine protection. The book concludes with the suitors lying in wait at the island of Asteris, ready to intercept Telemachus’s ship.

People: Menelaus (king of Sparta, provides news of Odysseus), Helen (queen of Sparta, provides comfort and stories), Telemachus (seeks news of his father), Pisistratus (son of Nestor, companion to Telemachus), Penelope (grieving mother, fears for her son), Antinous (suitor, leads the plot to kill Telemachus), Proteus (sea-god, reveals the fates of the Achaeans), Athena (goddess, guides and protects Telemachus).

Ideas: Xenia, Nostos, Fate, Loyalty.

## Book 5: Calypso—Ulysses Reaches Scheria on a Raft

The gods convene on Olympus, where Athena pleads for the release of Odysseus, who languishes on Ogygia under the nymph Calypso’s forced affection. Zeus agrees and dispatches Hermes to command Calypso to let him go. Though she resents the gods’ jealousy of her mortal lovers, Calypso obeys. She finds Odysseus weeping on the shore and instructs him to build a raft. Odysseus, suspicious of her motives, demands an oath before he begins construction. After four days of labor, he departs, sailing for seventeen days until he sights Scheria. Poseidon, returning from Ethiopia, spots him and, enraged, summons a catastrophic storm that shatters the raft. Odysseus is saved by the sea-goddess Ino, who gives him an enchanted veil to keep him afloat. After two days of drifting, he reaches the rocky coast of Scheria. Athena calms the winds, allowing him to swim to a river mouth. Exhausted and battered, he casts Ino’s veil into the sea, crawls into a thicket of olive trees, and falls into a deep, restorative sleep provided by Athena.

People: Odysseus (suffering hero), Athena (his divine protector), Zeus (king of gods), Calypso (nymph who detains him), Hermes (divine messenger), Poseidon (his divine antagonist), Ino (sea-goddess who aids him).

Ideas: Nostos (homecoming), Divine Intervention, Fate, Xenia (in the context of Calypso's forced hospitality).

QUOTES:

## Book 6: The Meeting Between Nausicaa and Ulysses

Athena appears in a dream to Nausicaa, daughter of King Alcinous of the Phaeacians, urging her to wash her clothes at the river to prepare for her impending marriage. The next morning, Nausicaa obtains her father’s permission and travels to the river with her maids. After washing the linen, they play ball, but Athena causes the ball to fall into the water, waking the shipwrecked Odysseus, who has been sleeping nearby. Terrified by his disheveled appearance, the maids flee, but Nausicaa stands firm. Odysseus addresses her with eloquent, respectful flattery, comparing her to a goddess and begging for aid. Nausicaa, showing wisdom and hospitality, provides him with clothes and oil for bathing. After he cleans himself, Athena enhances his appearance, making him look taller and more handsome. Nausicaa, struck by his beauty, instructs him to follow her to the city but to wait in a grove dedicated to Athena before approaching her parents’ palace, fearing local gossip. Odysseus follows her directions and prays to Athena for a kind reception, though the goddess remains hidden to avoid the wrath of Poseidon.

People: Athena (guides events to aid Odysseus), Nausicaa (kind, brave princess who helps Odysseus), Alcinous (king of the Phaeacians), Odysseus (the shipwrecked hero seeking home).

Ideas: Xenia (the duty to aid strangers), Appearance vs. Reality (Athena’s transformation of Odysseus), Social Propriety (Nausicaa’s concern for her reputation).

QUOTES:

## Book 7: Reception of Ulysses at the Palace of King Alcinous

Athena, disguised as a young girl, guides Odysseus through the city of the Phaeacians, shielding him in a protective mist. She advises him to seek the favor of Queen Arete, whose wisdom and influence are paramount in the court of King Alcinous. Odysseus marvels at the opulent palace, with its bronze walls, gold doors, and enchanted gardens that bloom eternally. He enters the hall and, as the mist dissipates, throws himself at Arete’s knees as a suppliant. Alcinous, prompted by the elder Echeneus, graciously welcomes the stranger, offering him a seat and food. During the meal, Alcinous wonders if his guest is a god, but Odysseus clarifies his mortal status and his desperate need to return home. He recounts his departure from Calypso’s island, his shipwreck, and his encounter with Nausicaa. Impressed by his character, Alcinous offers Odysseus his daughter’s hand in marriage and a safe passage home. Odysseus prays to Zeus that the King fulfills this promise. The Phaeacians retire, and Odysseus is shown to a comfortable bed, finally finding rest after his long, arduous journey.

People: Odysseus (suppliant seeking return), Athena (protector guiding him), Alcinous (hospitable King), Arete (wise Queen), Nausicaa (King's daughter who helped him).

Ideas: Xenia (hospitality to strangers), Nostos (the longing for home), Divine Intervention (Athena’s guidance), Wealth and Civilization (the Phaeacian luxury).

## Book 8: The Games and the Bard

Athena, disguised as a herald, gathers the Phaeacians to honor the stranger. Alcinous prepares a ship and a feast, inviting the bard Demodocus. During the banquet, Demodocus sings of the quarrel between Odysseus and Achilles, causing Odysseus to weep behind his mantle. To cheer their guest, Alcinous initiates athletic games. When Euryalus taunts Odysseus for his lack of participation, a stung Odysseus throws a massive disc far beyond the others and challenges the Phaeacians to any contest. Alcinous defuses the tension, praising his people’s skill in dance and song instead. Demodocus then sings of the illicit love between Ares and Aphrodite, trapped by Hephaestus’s invisible chains, which delights the gods. After a dance exhibition, Euryalus apologizes with a gift of a sword. Odysseus bathes and bids a poignant farewell to Nausicaa. During the final feast, Odysseus asks Demodocus to sing of the Trojan Horse. As the bard recounts the fall of Troy, Odysseus weeps uncontrollably. Alcinous notices his distress, stops the music, and finally demands that the stranger reveal his true name, his home, and the reason for his sorrow.

People: Alcinous (generous king), Odysseus (grieving hero), Athena (divine helper), Demodocus (blind bard), Euryalus (arrogant athlete), Nausicaa (kind princess).

Ideas: Xenia, Nostos, Metis, The power of song, Identity.

QUOTES:

## Book 9: The Cyclops

Odysseus reveals his identity to King Alcinous and recounts his journey from Troy. After sacking Ismarus, his men’s refusal to leave leads to a disastrous conflict with the Cicons. Driven by storms, they reach the land of the Lotus-eaters, where Odysseus must forcibly rescue crewmen addicted to the forgetful lotus. They then arrive at the island of the Cyclopes, a lawless, uncivilized race. Odysseus takes twelve men to explore the cave of Polyphemus, a giant son of Poseidon. Trapped by a massive stone, the men are imprisoned as the monster devours them two by two. Odysseus uses his *metis*, plying the giant with potent wine and claiming his name is "Noman." When Polyphemus falls into a drunken stupor, Odysseus and his men blind the monster’s single eye with a heated olive-wood stake. The other Cyclopes, hearing Polyphemus cry that "Noman" is hurting him, depart. Odysseus escapes by clinging to the bellies of the giant’s rams. Once at sea, his hubris compels him to reveal his true name, prompting Polyphemus to pray to his father, Poseidon, for a cursed, lonely, and delayed homecoming for the hero.

People: Odysseus (narrator, clever survivor), Alcinous (Phaeacian king), Polyphemus (Cyclops, Poseidon’s son), Cicons (warriors), Lotus-eaters (passive hosts), Poseidon (god of the sea, vengeful father).

Ideas: Metis (cunning), Hubris (pride), Xenia (violated hospitality), Nostos (homecoming).

QUOTES:

- (9.17) "We Cyclopes do not care about Jove or any of your blessed gods, for we are ever so much stronger than they."
- (9.28) "But Polyphemus shouted to them from inside the cave, ‘Noman is killing me by fraud; no man is killing me by force.’"
- (9.38) "‘Cyclops, if any one asks you who it was that put your eye out and spoiled your beauty, say it was the valiant warrior Ulysses, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca.’"

## Book 10: Aeolus, the Laestrygones, and Circe

Odysseus recounts his journey to the island of Aeolus, who gifts him a sack containing the winds to ensure a safe voyage home. Within sight of Ithaca, Odysseus’s crew, driven by greed and suspicion, open the sack, unleashing a storm that blows them back to Aeolus. The god, now convinced Odysseus is cursed by heaven, refuses further aid. The fleet reaches the land of the Laestrygonians, giant cannibals who destroy eleven ships and devour the crews. Only Odysseus’s ship escapes. They arrive at Aeaea, home of the goddess Circe. After Odysseus kills a stag to feed his men, he sends Eurylochus to explore. Circe transforms the scouting party into swine. Protected by the herb Moly, a gift from Hermes, Odysseus confronts Circe, forces her to restore his men, and becomes her lover. After a year of luxury, the crew urges departure. Circe agrees but mandates a terrifying detour: Odysseus must travel to the underworld to consult the prophet Teiresias. As they prepare to leave, the youngest crewman, Elpenor, falls from the roof and dies, leaving his body behind as the rest set sail for the realm of Hades.

People: Aeolus (god of winds, initially helpful, later dismissive), Antiphates (Laestrygonian king, cannibal giant), Circe (goddess/enchantress, transforms men, later a guide), Hermes (messenger god, provides Moly), Eurylochus (crewman, suspicious of Circe), Elpenor (young crewman, dies accidentally).

Ideas: Temptation, Metis (cunning), Fate, Xenia (violated by Laestrygonians and Circe).

QUOTES:

## Book 11: The Visit to the Dead

Odysseus sails to the land of the Cimmerians, where he performs a blood sacrifice to summon the spirits of the underworld. He first encounters his unburied comrade Elpenor, who begs for a proper funeral. Odysseus then meets the prophet Teiresias, who warns him that Poseidon will plague his journey because he blinded Polyphemus. Teiresias instructs Odysseus to avoid harming the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia; if he fails, his ship and crew will perish. He also predicts that Odysseus will eventually return home to kill the suitors, perform a final sacrifice to Poseidon, and die a peaceful death in old age. Odysseus speaks with his mother, Anticlea, who reveals the grief of his family in Ithaca. He then interviews various heroines and hears the tragic tale of Agamemnon’s murder by Clytemnestra. Achilles appears, lamenting the misery of death, but finds comfort in news of his son Neoptolemus’s bravery. Ajax remains silent, still bitter over the contest for Achilles’ armor. Finally, Odysseus witnesses the eternal punishments of Tityus, Tantalus, and Sisyphus before fleeing the underworld in fear of the Gorgon’s head, returning to his ship to continue his journey.

People: Odysseus (seeks prophecy), Elpenor (unburied ghost), Teiresias (blind prophet), Anticlea (Odysseus's mother), Agamemnon (murdered king), Achilles (great warrior), Ajax (sullen hero), Hercules (phantom).

Ideas: Nostos, Fate, Hubris, The Finality of Death.

QUOTES:
- (11.36) "Aegisthus and my wicked wife were the death of me between them. He asked me to his house, feasted me, and then butchered me most miserably as though I were a fat beast."
- (11.42) "I would rather be a paid servant in a poor man’s house and be above ground than king of kings among the dead."
- (11.51) "I saw also the dreadful fate of Tantalus, who stood in a lake that reached his chin; he was dying to quench his thirst, but could never reach the water."

## Book 12: The Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, The Cattle of the Sun

After burying Elpenor on Aeaea, Odysseus receives final instructions from Circe. She warns him of the Sirens, the deadly Scylla and Charybdis, and the sacred cattle of the Sun. Following her advice, Odysseus plugs his crew’s ears with wax and has himself lashed to the mast to survive the Sirens' song. They then navigate the straits; while avoiding the whirlpool Charybdis, the monster Scylla snatches six men from the ship. Exhausted and starving, the crew lands on Thrinacia. Despite Odysseus’s warnings and their oaths, Eurylochus convinces the men to slaughter the Sun-god’s cattle while Odysseus sleeps. When they set sail, Zeus destroys their ship with a thunderbolt as retribution. All the men perish at sea, leaving only Odysseus. He drifts back through the straits, narrowly escaping Charybdis by clinging to a fig tree, and is eventually washed ashore on Ogygia, where Calypso rescues him. Odysseus concludes his long narrative to the Phaeacians, ending his account of his wanderings.

People: Odysseus (narrator, sole survivor), Circe (divine guide), Sirens (enchantresses), Scylla (six-headed monster), Charybdis (whirlpool), Eurylochus (mutinous crewman), Hyperion (Sun-god), Zeus (punisher), Calypso (goddess who rescues Odysseus).

Ideas: Temptation, divine retribution, the cost of leadership, fate vs. free will, the dangers of hubris.

QUOTES:
- (12.5) "If any one unwarily draws in too close and hears the singing of the Sirens, his wife and children will never welcome him home again."
- (12.20) "As a fisherman, seated, spear in hand, upon some jutting rock throws bait into the water to deceive the poor little fishes, and spears them."
- (12.38) "A jury-man is not more glad to get home to supper, after having been long detained in court by troublesome cases, than I was to see my raft."

## Book 13: Ulysses Leaves Scheria and Returns to Ithaca

The Phaeacians host a final feast for Odysseus, who eagerly awaits sunset to depart. After exchanging gifts and final farewells, he boards their ship and falls into a deep, deathlike sleep. The Phaeacian crew rows with supernatural speed, reaching Ithaca by dawn. They deposit the sleeping Odysseus and his treasures near the cave of the Naiads before departing. Enraged by the Phaeacians' assistance, Poseidon complains to Zeus, who permits him to punish them. Poseidon turns their ship into stone as it nears Scheria, fulfilling an ancient prophecy. Meanwhile, Odysseus wakes in Ithaca but fails to recognize his home due to a mist cast by Athena. He initially suspects the Phaeacians have abandoned him elsewhere. Athena appears disguised as a shepherd, eventually revealing her identity and clearing the mist. She confirms his location and advises him on how to reclaim his house from the suitors. To ensure his safety, Athena transforms Odysseus into an aged, ragged beggar. She instructs him to seek out Eumaeus, his loyal swineherd, while she travels to Sparta to summon Telemachus home.

People: Odysseus (returns home, disguised as a beggar), Athena (guides and transforms him), Poseidon (punishes the Phaeacians), Alcinous (Phaeacian king who aids Odysseus), Eumaeus (loyal swineherd).

Ideas: Nostos, Metis, Divine Intervention, Fate.

## Book 14: Ulysses in the Hut with Eumaeus

Odysseus arrives at the hut of Eumaeus, his loyal swineherd, disguised as an old beggar. Eumaeus welcomes him with traditional xenia, despite his own poverty and the burden of feeding the suitors who occupy Odysseus's palace. Over a meal of pork, Eumaeus laments the loss of his master and expresses deep skepticism regarding rumors of Odysseus's survival. Odysseus, maintaining his disguise, fabricates a complex backstory—claiming to be a Cretan veteran of the Trojan War who survived various shipwrecks and travels through Egypt and Thesprotia. He swears a solemn oath that Odysseus will return within the month to exact vengeance. Eumaeus remains unconvinced but continues to treat the stranger with kindness, honoring the protection of Zeus. As night falls, the men eat and share stories. Odysseus tests Eumaeus’s generosity by recounting a tale from the Trojan War about needing a cloak, prompting the swineherd to provide him with a warm covering for the night. Eumaeus then leaves to sleep near his herd, demonstrating his unwavering dedication to his master’s property.

People: Odysseus (disguised as a beggar, testing loyalty), Eumaeus (loyal, hospitable swineherd), Mesaulius (a servant purchased by Eumaeus).

Ideas: Xenia, Loyalty, Disguise, Fate.

QUOTES:

## Book 15: The Return of Telemachus

Athena travels to Lacedaemon to urge Telemachus to return home, warning him of the suitors' ambush in the strait and advising him to visit Eumaeus first. After receiving lavish gifts from Menelaus and Helen, Telemachus departs with Pisistratus.
